Part 1 · The problem

3 arrows, 3 hits. Does she never miss?

3arrows shot by the new Mirana
3hits
0misses
1.00MLE hit rate: “she never misses”

The MLE (hits ÷ tries) says θ̂ = 3/3 = 1. If you believed it, you would predict that the next arrow hits with probability 100%. Nobody in Dota believes that after three arrows. The MLE overfits: with little data it trusts the data too much. The course calls this the zero-count problem or the black swan paradox: you never saw a miss, so the MLE says a miss is impossible.

What does an experienced player do instead? They already have a feeling: “most Miranas hit about half their arrows”. Three hits move that feeling up, but not to 100%. That feeling before the data is called the prior. Combining it with the data gives the posterior. Today we make this exact.

MLEafter 3 arrows

Hit rate is 1.00. She will never miss. Ever.

Bayesianafter 3 arrows

I thought “about 0.5”. Three hits push me up to about 0.71. Ask me again after 30 arrows.

Remember it: MLE uses only the data. Bayes uses the data and what you believed before. With little data, that makes a big difference.

Part 2 · Toolbox

Five tools. Each with a tiny example.

Tool 1 · Bayes' rule for a parameter

Turn “data given θ” into “θ given data”

p(θ | D) = p(D | θ) · p(θ) / p(D)

p(θ) prior: belief about θ before the data. p(D | θ) likelihood: how probable the data is if θ were true. p(θ | D) posterior: belief after the data. p(D): one number that makes the area 1. This exact formula was asked on the 2026 final, Task 1(a).

Tool 2 · “∝” means “proportional to”

Ignore constants until the very end

p(θ | D) ∝ p(D | θ) · p(θ)

p(D) does not contain θ, so it cannot change the shape of the curve. We drop it and write ∝. Same for any factor without θ, like C(n, k). Only factors with θ matter.

Try it: which factors can you drop from 6 · θ³ · (1 − θ) · 0.2?

6 and 0.2 (no θ). What stays: θ³(1 − θ).

Tool 3 · Powers add up

Same base: add the exponents

θa · θb = θa+b

θ² · θ³ = θ·θ · θ·θ·θ = θ⁵. Same for (1 − θ). This one rule does all the work today.

Try it: θ⁴(1 − θ)² · θ(1 − θ)⁵ = ?

θ⁵(1 − θ)⁷.

Tool 4 · Density

A curve whose area gives probability

θ can be any number between 0 and 1, so our belief is a curve, a density. Like GPM: it is not gold itself, gold = GPM × minutes. Probability = density × width = area. A density can be higher than 1; only areas must stay ≤ 1.

Tool 5 · The Beta distribution, recognised by its shape

Beta(A, B) is any curve shaped like θA−1(1 − θ)B−1 on [0, 1]

Beta(θ | A, B) = θA−1 (1 − θ)B−1 / B(A, B)

B(A, B) is only the number that makes the area 1; you never compute it by hand. The key skill: when you see θ4(1 − θ)1, read off the exponents and add 1: that is Beta(5, 2). Think of A as “hits + 1” and B as “misses + 1”. Mean: A/(A + B).

Try it: which Beta has the shape θ⁹(1 − θ)⁴⁹?

Beta(10, 50). Exponent + 1 each. Its mean is 10/60 ≈ 0.167 (remember this one, it was on the 2025 final).

Remember it: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; drop factors without θ; add exponents; read the Beta off the exponents (+1).

Part 3 · The three ingredients

Likelihood, prior, posterior. One step each.

Likelihood

What the data says

k hits and n − k misses: θk(1 − θ)n−k. The Binomial's C(n, k) has no θ, so it drops (Tool 2).

Prior

What you believed before

A Beta(a, b): θa−1(1 − θ)b−1. Same shape as a likelihood, as if you had already seen a − 1 hits and b − 1 misses.

Posterior

What you believe now

Multiply them. The shapes merge into one Beta. Derivation in Part 4.

Choosing a prior: a, b are “pseudo-counts”
priorfeels like having seenmean a/(a+b)meaning
Beta(1, 1)0 hits, 0 misses0.5flat: “no idea at all”
Beta(2, 2)1 hit, 1 miss0.5gentle: “probably around half”
Beta(20, 20)19 hits, 19 misses0.5strong: “I'm quite sure it's about half”
Beta(8, 4)7 hits, 3 misses0.67“good Miranas hit about 2 of 3”

The sum a + b is the strength of the prior (the course calls it the effective sample size): how many arrows your belief is “worth”.

Remember it: the prior Beta(a, b) is like having already watched a − 1 hits and b − 1 misses. Bigger a + b = stronger belief.

Part 4 · The derivation, every line with its reason

Prior Beta(a, b) + k hits, n − k misses

Tool 2p(θ | D) ∝ p(D | θ) · p(θ)Bayes' rule without the constant p(D)
plug in∝ C(n,k) θk(1 − θ)n−k · θa−1(1 − θ)b−1 / B(a,b)likelihood (Binomial) times prior (Beta)
drop∝ θk(1 − θ)n−k · θa−1(1 − θ)b−1C(n, k) and B(a, b) contain no θ
Tool 3= θa+k−1 (1 − θ)b+n−k−1add exponents of θ, and of (1 − θ)
Tool 5θ | D ~ Beta(a + k, b + n − k)read the Beta off the exponents (+1)

In words: add the hits to a, add the misses to b. That's the whole update. The prior parameters really behave like counts, which is why they are called pseudo-counts.

Conjugate prior: the prior was a Beta and the posterior is again a Beta, just with new numbers. A prior with this property is called conjugate to the likelihood. That is why we chose the Beta: the math stays one line long.

Our Mirana: prior Beta(2, 2), data 3 hits, 0 misses:

Beta(2 + 3, 2 + 0) = Beta(5, 2)

Order doesn't matter: update after each arrow or all at once, you end at the same Beta. Adding counts one at a time or in a batch gives the same total.

the only formula
to memorise today
a + k, b + n − k

Remember it: Beta prior × Binomial likelihood = Beta posterior. Hits go to a, misses go to b.

Part 6 · Three numbers from one posterior

Mean, MAP, MLE. Why are they different?

The posterior is a whole curve. Often you need one number. There are three common choices. Write the posterior as Beta(A, B) with A = a + k, B = b + n − k.

Posterior mean5/7 ≈ 0.71A / (A + B)

The balance point of the curve. Uses the whole belief. This is also the probability that the next arrow hits.

MAP (the peak)4/5 = 0.80(A − 1) / (A + B − 2)

The single most believable θ. “Maximum a posteriori”: the top of the posterior curve.

MLE3/3 = 1.00k / n

Ignores the prior completely. Only the data.

Numbers for our Mirana: prior Beta(2, 2), 3 hits, 0 misses → posterior Beta(5, 2).

Where does the MAP formula come from? (Week 2, Question 1)

loglog p(θ | D) = (A − 1) log θ + (B − 1) log(1 − θ) + constlog of the Beta shape; B(A, B) becomes a constant
derivative(A − 1)/θ − (B − 1)/(1 − θ) = 0top of the hill: slope 0
× θ(1−θ)(A − 1)(1 − θ) = (B − 1)θclear both fractions
solveθ̂MAP = (A − 1)/(A + B − 2)expand, collect θ (works when A, B > 1)

Notice: this is exactly the MLE calculation, with “hits” = A − 1 and “misses” = B − 1. The MAP is the MLE of the data plus the prior's pseudo-counts. With a flat prior Beta(1, 1) the MAP equals the MLE.

Part 7 · The compromise

The posterior mean sits between belief and data.

Write the posterior mean in a clever way. Prior mean m = a/(a + b), MLE k/n, and the weight

λ = (a + b) / (a + b + n)

Then the posterior mean is a weighted average:

E[θ | D] = λ · m + (1 − λ) · k/n

λ is “how many pseudo-arrows the prior is worth, out of all arrows”. With few real arrows, λ is big and the prior dominates. With many arrows, λ goes to 0 and the data wins. Belief is never stuck; it only needs enough evidence.

weightλ = 4/(4 + 3) = 4/7prior Beta(2, 2) is worth 4 arrows; 3 real arrows
mix(4/7)·0.5 + (3/7)·1 = 2/7 + 3/7prior mean 0.5, MLE 1
result= 5/7 ≈ 0.71 ✓same as A/(A + B)
300 arrowsλ = 4/304 ≈ 0.013after 300 arrows the prior barely matters

Remember it: posterior mean = weighted average of prior mean and MLE; the prior's weight is (a + b) out of (a + b + n).

Part 8 · Uncertainty and prediction

How sure are we? And what happens next?

Error bar

Uncertainty shrinks like 1/√n

The posterior variance is AB / ((A + B)²(A + B + 1)). With lots of data it is about

sd ≈ √( θ̂(1 − θ̂) / n )

70 hits of 100: sd ≈ √(0.21/100) ≈ 0.046. 700 of 1000: sd ≈ 0.014. Ten times more arrows make the error bar about three times smaller (√10 ≈ 3.2). To halve the error you need four times the data.

Posterior predictive

P(next arrow hits) = posterior mean

p(x̃ = 1 | D) = ∫ θ · p(θ | D) dθ = A/(A + B)

In words: average “chance of a hit” over everything you still believe. For Beta(5, 2): 5/7 ≈ 0.71, not 1.

Laplace's rule of succession (flat prior a = b = 1): (k + 1)/(n + 2). Zero hits in 3 tries gives 1/5 = 0.2, never exactly 0. That is “add-one smoothing”, the fix for the zero-count problem.

Bonus · two arrows in a row (Week 3, Question 2)

Multiply step by step, updating in between

P(next two hit) = P(first hits) × P(second hits | first hit) = A/(A + B) · (A + 1)/(A + B + 1). For Beta(5, 2): 5/7 · 6/8 ≈ 0.54. After the first hit, your belief is already Beta(6, 2), so the second factor uses the updated counts.

Remember it: more data → narrower posterior (sd ~ 1/√n). The chance of the next hit is the posterior mean, A/(A + B).

Explain it back. Answer out loud before opening.

What are the prior, the likelihood and the posterior, in one sentence each?

Prior: what I believed about θ before the data. Likelihood: how well each θ explains the data. Posterior: my belief after combining both, ∝ likelihood × prior.

Why do the hits get added to a?

The prior has θa−1, the data has θk. Multiplying adds the exponents: θa+k−1, which is a Beta with first parameter a + k.

What does “conjugate prior” mean?

Prior and posterior are in the same family (Beta in, Beta out), so updating only changes the numbers.

Why is the posterior mean between the prior mean and the MLE?

It is a weighted average of them. The prior's weight is (a + b)/(a + b + n), which shrinks as you get more data.

How does Bayes fix the 3-out-of-3 problem?

The prior's pseudo-misses stay in the count, so the posterior never says “impossible to miss”. With Beta(2, 2): next-hit chance 5/7, not 1.

Practice · 30–40 min

Practice problems. Paper first.

01Easy

Pudge hooks

Prior for Pudge's hook rate: Beta(2, 2). He throws 4 hooks: hit, miss, hit, hit. Find the posterior, its mean, the MAP and the MLE.

Hint

k = 3 hits, n − k = 1 miss. Add them to a and b.

Solution
  • Posterior Beta(2 + 3, 2 + 1) = Beta(5, 3)
  • Mean 5/8 = 0.625; MAP (5 − 1)/(8 − 2) = 4/6 ≈ 0.667; MLE 3/4 = 0.75
  • Order: mean < MAP < MLE here, all pulled towards the prior's 0.5 except the MLE.
02Easy

Weak or strong belief

(a) Which Beta means “no idea at all”? (b) Beta(2, 2) and Beta(20, 20) have the same mean. What is it, and how are they different? (c) Which one moves more after 5 hits in a row?

Hint

a + b is how many arrows the prior is worth.

Solution
  • (a) Beta(1, 1), flat
  • (b) Mean 0.5 for both. Beta(20, 20) is worth 40 arrows, so it is much narrower (more certain).
  • (c) Beta(2, 2) → Beta(7, 2), mean 0.78. Beta(20, 20) → Beta(25, 20), mean 0.56. The weak prior moves much more.
03Medium

Invoker's Sunstrikes

A new Invoker lands 0 of 3 Sunstrikes. (a) MLE of the hit rate? What does it predict for the next one? (b) With a flat prior, what is P(next Sunstrike hits)?

Hint

Laplace's rule: (k + 1)/(n + 2).

Solution
  • (a) 0/3 = 0: “the next one can never hit”. The zero-count problem.
  • (b) Posterior Beta(1, 4); P = 1/5 = 0.2.
04Medium

The compromise formula

Prior Beta(6, 4). Then 30 hits in 40 arrows. Find the posterior mean twice: directly, and with λ·m + (1 − λ)·MLE.

Hint

m = 6/10, MLE = 30/40, λ = 10/50.

Solution
  • Posterior Beta(36, 14), mean 36/50 = 0.72
  • λ = 0.2: 0.2 · 0.6 + 0.8 · 0.75 = 0.12 + 0.60 = 0.72 ✓
05Medium

Day by day or all at once

Flat prior. Day 1: 2 hits, 1 miss. Day 2: 1 hit, 3 misses. Update after day 1, then again after day 2. Then do it in one step with all data. Compare. What is P(next hit)?

Hint

Yesterday's posterior is today's prior.

Solution
  • After day 1: Beta(3, 2). After day 2: Beta(4, 5).
  • All at once: 3 hits, 4 misses → Beta(1 + 3, 1 + 4) = Beta(4, 5). Same ✓
  • P(next hit) = 4/9 ≈ 0.44
06Exam-style · 2025 Final 3

Losses before the first win

p(k | q) = (1 − q)k q, prior Beta(a, b). (a) Derive the posterior for data k₁, …, kₙ. (b) a = b = 1, data 2, 0, 3, 1: posterior and P(you win tomorrow's first game)? (c) If the posterior is Beta(10, 50), what is p(0 | D)?

Hint

Likelihood qⁿ(1 − q)^Σk. P(k = 0 | q) = q, so p(0 | D) is the posterior mean.

Solution
  • (a) qa+n−1(1 − q)b+Σk−1 → Beta(a + n, b + Σk)
  • (b) n = 4, Σk = 6 → Beta(5, 7); P = 5/12 ≈ 0.42
  • (c) 10/60 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167
07Exam-style · 2026 Final 2

Games until the first win

p(y | λ) = λ(1 − λ)y−1, y = 1, 2, …, prior Beta(2, 2). Data: 3, 1, 2. Derive and compute the posterior and its mean.

Hint

Likelihood λⁿ(1 − λ)^(Σy − n).

Solution
  • Posterior Beta(a + n, b + Σy − n) = Beta(2 + 3, 2 + 6 − 3) = Beta(5, 5)
  • Mean 5/10 = 0.5
08Challenge · Week 3 Q2

Two arrows in a row

Posterior Beta(5, 2). (a) P(the next two arrows both hit)? (b) Compare with the “plug-in” answer (5/7)². Which is bigger, and why?

Hint

After the first hit the belief becomes Beta(6, 2).

Solution
  • (a) 5/7 · 6/8 = 30/56 ≈ 0.536
  • (b) (5/7)² ≈ 0.510. The Bayesian answer is bigger: a first hit makes you believe more in a high θ, which raises the chance of the second.
